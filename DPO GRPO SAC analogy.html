What the analogy is honest about

Every policy here is a Gaussian over a single continuous action, so ∇ log π, the KL and the DPO margin all have closed forms — the forces drawn on screen are the real gradients of the real objectives for this family, not sketches. The DPO margin really is linear in θ here, which is why its implicit reward shows up as a straight line that can never grow a peak: with a Gaussian policy and a fixed pair, DPO has no way to represent “good in the middle”.

Three things are deliberate simplifications. GRPO’s clip is drawn as a travel stop on the force rather than a ratio clip on each token, because there are no tokens here. SAC’s critic is a kernel-smoothed table over the action axis instead of a neural network, and it has no bootstrapping: with one state and no transitions, Q is just the fitted reward. And the three forces share one gain η so that they move at comparable speed on screen — the race is about where each force points, never about which converges faster in wall-clock terms.

What to take away

Turn the temperature up and SAC’s blob inflates until the ball is rolling on a blurred version of the landscape rather than the real one; that blur is what lets it leave the small hill, and it is also why too much of it parks the policy in a valley between peaks. Set the landscape flat and GRPO has nothing: identical rewards in a group give identically zero advantages, however large you make G. DPO keeps working on that flat landscape, which is the point of preference data — and keeps working just as happily when the pins point somewhere bad, which is the cost.
